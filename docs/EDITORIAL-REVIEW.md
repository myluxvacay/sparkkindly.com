### A day in shadows
Route: /discover/a-day-in-shadows/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

The quiet movement at your feet can reveal a little about the sky.

#### Notice the light

A shadow forms where an object blocks light. On a sunny day, a fixed object’s shadow can change as the Sun appears to move across the sky. Looking at the shadow lets you observe the light without looking directly at the Sun.

Use the same object and the same level surface each time. A moving object or a sloping surface can make comparisons harder to interpret.

#### Follow a changing angle

When the Sun is low in the sky, a vertical object often casts a long shadow. As the Sun gets higher, that shadow becomes shorter. Its direction changes as well.

The shortest shadow is around local solar noon, which need not be 12:00 on a clock. Location, season, and the clock system all matter. A cloudy day can make a shadow difficult to see.

#### Keep an observation notebook

Make two or three drawings at different times. Label the time, the weather, and which way the shadow points. Describe what you actually saw before trying to explain it.

Never look directly at the Sun. If you investigate outside, choose a safe place away from roads and use ordinary sun protection. Children should work with an adult.

Takeaway: Watch one fixed object at different times. Changing sunlight can change both the length and direction of its shadow.

Source: [NASA: I Tell Only Sunny Hours](https://science.gsfc.nasa.gov/attic/sunearthday.nasa.gov/2005/images/Sun_Dial_pdf.pdf)

### A smaller next step
Route: /grow/a-smaller-next-step/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

Turn “I should do that” into one concrete action you can see.

#### Name the task

Some tasks sound small until we try to start them. “Sort the cupboard” contains many decisions: where to begin, what to keep, and where everything should go. Write the task down in plain language.

Choose just one part. You might pick the top shelf or a single drawer. Defining a boundary gives you a clear place to stop as well as a place to start.

#### Make the next action visible

Replace “get organised” with “put the empty boxes together.” An action is easier to recognise when you can imagine what your hands will do. If it still feels vague, divide it again.

Prepare only what that step needs. For the cupboard, that might mean a clear patch of floor and one bag. Avoid making a new project out of preparing for the old one.

#### Decide when enough is enough

Choose a stopping point before beginning. After one shelf, you can continue, take a break, or leave the rest for another day. Finishing a small part is still a real change.

This is a practical planning suggestion, not a promise of productivity or wellbeing. Tasks differ, and asking someone for practical help may be the useful next step.

Takeaway: Choose one visible action and a stopping point that makes sense for today.

### Beneath the blue
Route: /discover/beneath-the-blue/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

An introduction to the ocean’s layers, one change in light at a time.

#### A world shaped by light

The ocean is not evenly lit from top to bottom. NOAA describes a sunlit upper region, a twilight region, and deeper water without sunlight. These are useful guides, rather than painted boundaries in the sea.

Significant sunlight is rarely present below about 200 metres. Water clarity and other conditions affect how far the light travels.

#### Into the twilight

The twilight zone extends roughly from 200 to 1,000 metres. A little sunlight can reach this region under the right conditions, but not enough for photosynthesis.

Below about 1,000 metres is the aphotic zone, where sunlight does not reach. The deep ocean includes further depth divisions; “midnight” names the bathypelagic region from about 1,000 to 4,000 metres.

#### Read a diagram with care

A simple layer diagram compresses enormous distances into a few bands of colour. Its colours are a teaching aid, not a photograph of what a diver would see.

When you meet a dramatic ocean image, ask whether it is a photograph, a diagram, or an illustration. Each can help us learn, but they tell us different kinds of things.

Takeaway: Use sunlight as your first guide to ocean layers, and read approximate depths as guides rather than hard borders.

Source: [NOAA: How far does light travel in the ocean?](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/light_travel.html)

### A five-minute pause
Route: /grow/five-minute-pause/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

A gentle reflection for an ordinary day. A notebook is optional.

#### Arrive as you are

This is an optional writing prompt, not a test. Choose a comfortable place and give yourself about five minutes if you have them. Keep your eyes open or simply look around. You can stop whenever you want.

Start with one neutral observation: the colour of a wall, the sound of a passing car, or the feeling of a cup in your hand. You do not need to achieve a particular mood.

#### Ask three small questions

What caught my attention today? Write a sentence about something specific, however ordinary. A conversation, a sentence in a book, or a patch of light is enough.

What took more effort than I expected? Name the task without turning it into a judgment about yourself. Then ask: what is one thing I would like to carry into tomorrow?

#### Choose what to keep

Read back only if you want to. Circle one phrase, close the notebook, or leave the page unfinished. There is no score and no correct answer.

This reflection offers a structure for thinking, not a health outcome. If reflecting feels unhelpful, choose a different activity or leave it for another day. Nothing needs to be entered on this website.

Takeaway: Notice one thing, name one effort, and choose one thought to carry forward.

### Five ways to give someone your attention
Route: /together/give-your-attention/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

Small, ordinary invitations to be a little more present.

#### Make a little space

1. Ask whether now is a good time. Attention is easier to offer when both people can choose the moment. “Would you like to talk?” leaves room for a yes, a no, or a later.

2. Put one distraction aside. You might silence your own phone or turn away from a screen, if doing so is practical. Do not demand that someone else do the same.

#### Let the other person lead

3. Ask an open question. “What was the interesting part?” can invite a fuller answer than a rapid series of guesses. Let silence be part of the conversation.

4. Check what you heard. Try “Have I understood that correctly?” rather than assuming you know how someone feels. People can correct your understanding in their own words.

#### Finish thoughtfully

5. Ask what would be useful. Someone may want company, an idea, practical help, or simply to finish a sentence. Accept their answer, including a wish for space.

These are everyday conversation suggestions, not therapy or a promise to repair a relationship. Your own boundaries matter too. You can offer attention without agreeing to do everything asked of you.

Takeaway: Ask, listen, check your understanding, and respect the other person’s choice.

### Number Detective
Route: /play/number-detective/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

A familiar sequence, a new way to see it. Can you find the next number?

#### Start with a rule

Here is your puzzle: using n × (n + 1), starting at n = 1, what comes after 2, 6, 12, 20? Take a moment before reading on. There is no clock to beat.

An unrestricted number sequence can have many possible continuations. This puzzle gives you its rule, so you can test one specific idea rather than guess what the author intended.

#### Look at the building blocks

Write the first four terms as products: 1 × 2, 2 × 3, 3 × 4, and 4 × 5. Each term multiplies a number by the number immediately after it.

The next product is 5 × 6, which makes 30. You can check it a second way: the gaps are 4, 6, 8, and then 10. Adding 10 to 20 gives the same answer.

#### Make a puzzle of your own

Choose a starting value for n and write three consecutive products using the same rule. Give a friend both the terms and the rule, then ask for the next product.

Try explaining why your answer works without saying “it just looks right.” Finding a pattern is fun; explaining it gives someone else a way to follow your thinking.

Materials: Paper, Pencil
Supervision: Independent activity for adults; children need an adult.
Safety: Use paper and pencils safely; no uploads or personal information needed.

Takeaway: The next term is 30: 5 × 6. A stated rule turns a guess into a checkable answer.

### One word, five clues
Route: /play/one-word-five-clues/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

Five carefully chosen clues. One everyday word. Take it one clue at a time.

#### The challenge

Choose one word from this set: planet, pencil, kettle, garden. It must satisfy every clue: it has six letters; it begins with P; its second letter is E; it names something you can hold; and it is used to make marks on paper.

You can solve this on paper. Cross out a word only when you can name a clue it fails. There is no need to use every clue in order.

#### Check each candidate

Planet begins with P and has six letters, but its second letter is L. Kettle starts with K. Garden starts with G. Pencil is the only candidate with P followed by E.

Pencil also has six letters, fits in a hand, and can make marks on paper. Within the supplied candidate set it is the unique solution. The set matters: we are not claiming to have checked every word in the English language.

#### Swap roles

Write down four new candidate words and choose a target. Build clues that eliminate the other three, checking each one before you share the puzzle.

Invite your partner to explain which clue did the most work. Sometimes one precise clue can be more helpful than a long list of vague ones.

Materials: Paper, Pencil
Supervision: Independent activity for adults; children need an adult.
Safety: Use paper and pencils safely; no uploads or personal information needed.

Takeaway: Pencil is the unique answer among the four supplied words.

### Shadow explorers
Route: /kids/shadow-explorers/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

For ages 6–9 with an adult: trace a shadow and see what changes.

#### Set up together

Ask an adult to choose a safe, flat outdoor spot away from roads. Place a small, sturdy toy on a sheet of paper in sunlight. Keep the paper from blowing away without using anything sharp.

The adult stays with you. Never look directly at the Sun, even for a moment. If it is too hot, wet, or uncomfortable, save the activity for another time.

#### Trace, wait, compare

Draw around the toy’s shadow. The adult can write the time next to your line. Leave the toy and paper in the same place and return together later.

Use a different colour to trace the new shadow. Has its direction or length changed? Clouds might make it faint. “We could not see a clear shadow” is a useful observation too.

#### Take the wonder off-screen

Talk about which line is longer. You can compare with a strip of paper instead of a ruler. Your drawing records what you saw; it does not have to look like anyone else’s.

For a screen-free extension, hold your hand near a wall while an adult shines an ordinary torch. Move the light, and describe how the shadow changes. Never shine the torch into anyone’s eyes.

Age guidance: Ages 6–9, with an adult

Materials: Paper, Coloured pencils, A sturdy toy
Supervision: An adult stays alongside throughout.
Safety: Never look directly at the Sun. Choose a safe location and suitable weather.

Takeaway: A shadow shows where light is blocked. Try changing the light and noticing what happens.

Source: [NASA: I Tell Only Sunny Hours](https://science.gsfc.nasa.gov/attic/sunearthday.nasa.gov/2005/images/Sun_Dial_pdf.pdf)

### The Little Things That Matter
Route: /together/the-little-things-that-matter/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

A fictional story about a rainy morning, a missed bus, and one small act of attention.

#### A rainy morning

Fiction · Pilot storyboard. Mara reached the bus stop just as the red tail lights disappeared around the corner. Rain slipped from the edge of her umbrella onto one shoe. She sighed and moved along the bench to make room for someone else.

An older neighbour stood nearby holding a paper bag. The bottom was beginning to soften. Mara offered the dry corner under her umbrella. “Your bag might need this more than I do,” she said.

#### A little room

The neighbour laughed and shifted closer. Inside the bag was a loaf of bread for a friend who had asked for company over breakfast. They talked about the bakery window, then about nothing in particular.

When the next bus came, Mara helped hold the bag while the neighbour found a ticket. Nobody gave a speech. Nobody called it a life-changing moment. It was simply a little less rain, shared between two people.

Takeaway: A small offer can be specific: a dry space, a free hand, a moment of attention.

### Words that help
Route: /kids/words-that-help/

Current status: draft. Reviewer: not recorded.

For ages 6–9 with an adult: match three everyday moments with thoughtful words.

#### Make the cards

Ask a grown-up to write three moments on separate pieces of paper: someone lends you a crayon; you accidentally knock over a tower; you want to join a game. No names or personal stories are needed.

On three more cards, write: “Thank you for sharing”; “I’m sorry. May I help rebuild it?”; and “May I join in?” Read all six cards together.

#### Try a match

Place a words card beside each moment. For the crayon, try “Thank you for sharing.” For the fallen tower, try an apology and an offer to help. To join a game, try asking first.

These are examples, not magic words. The other person can still say no. You can be disappointed and still respect their choice. Talk with your grown-up about another activity you could choose.

#### Practise away from the screen

Swap roles and act out a pretend scene using toys. You do not need to record it, upload it, or tell the website how it went. There is no score.

For a screen-free extension, draw a new imaginary moment and invent a helpful sentence together. Keep it kind and specific. The grown-up can help if any part feels confusing.

Age guidance: Ages 6–9, with an adult

Materials: Paper, Pencil
Supervision: An adult stays alongside throughout.
Safety: Use paper and pencils safely; no uploads or personal information needed.

Takeaway: Helpful words can thank, repair, or invite. Listening to the answer matters too.
